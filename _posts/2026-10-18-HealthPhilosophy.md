Everyone seems to have a philosophy about health. Mine can be stated simply: reduce sugar, and increase the nutrient density of what we eat.

I came to this practically, not theoretically. Seeking a diet that would be better for my mind and my mental health, I consulted a nutritionist, who recommended cutting out sugar and following a ketogenic approach. Today I follow a low-carb diet rather than a strict keto one, but the heart of her advice, removing sugar, has stayed with me. She also pointed me to *The Alzheimer's Antidote* by Amy Berger, which showed me that there is a better way.

In the years since, these changes have made a real difference to me. My mind is clearer, I have been more positive and more productive, and I have been healthier overall. That is only one person's experience, but it is the reason I take this philosophy seriously.

This philosophy is not Biblical or particularly religious, but the Bible does poetically describe a life of health that we can hope to achieve with the Lord's help. I believe these verses imply the wisdom of having a health strategy:

>Even youths grow tired and weary,  
>and young men stumble and fall;  
>but those who hope in the Lord  
>will renew their strength.  
>They will soar on wings like eagles;  
>they will run and not grow weary,  
>they will walk and not be faint.  
>
>—Isaiah 40:30-31 (NIV)

It makes sense to choose what unlocks the mind and maximizes healthspan: the longest healthy life possible. Not every illness can be avoided, but it is wise to do what we can to lead toward genuine health and happiness.

### Lessons from the blue zones

Dan Buettner has studied "blue zones," regions reported to have unusually high numbers of people who live past a hundred. The five he usually lists are Okinawa, Sardinia, Ikaria, Nicoya, and Loma Linda. It is not enough to *reach* old age; we should also hope to avoid the diseases that so often accompany it.

What stands out is what these communities do not eat. They eat plenty of carbohydrates, but mostly whole-food ones such as beans and whole grains, and they avoid processed foods and refined carbohydrates, of which sugar is a major part.

Personally, it seems straightforward that avoiding drugs and alcohol leads to greater health and protects the mind. Not everyone in the blue zones abstains from alcohol, but I believe abstaining is wise. There is one more item to consider removing, which has become a modern American drug of choice: sugar. Although sugar is deeply entrenched in American life, it is still possible to remove it and live with greater health.

### What sugar does

The most obvious effect of a high-sugar diet is weight gain, but that is not all. Sugar contributes to inflammation and to the quiet metabolic damage that eventually shows up as disease.

The effect on the mind concerns me most, and mainly over the long term. Alzheimer's disease is, as I understand it, largely a metabolic disorder of the brain's energy supply, an argument Amy Berger lays out in *The Alzheimer's Antidote*. The large-scale data point the same way. A UK Biobank analysis [found that higher intake of total sugar and free sugar was associated with higher dementia risk](https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/publications/association-of-sugar-intake-with-incident-dementia-in-the-uk-biobank-a-prospective-cohort-study/), an effect that was evident in women in that study but not in men. And a [2026 study in *Neurology*](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000218313) found that people who spent their earliest years under wartime and postwar sugar rationing developed dementia less often, with about 21–23% lower risk. These studies show association, not proof. Taken together with the mechanisms below, though, they suggest that what we eat bears on how our minds age.

Pediatric endocrinologist Robert Lustig [explains how this may work](https://foodforthebrain.org/is-sugar-killing-your-brain/). The more refined carbohydrate and sugar we eat, the more resistant we become to insulin. Ironically, that can make it harder for the brain to take up the glucose it needs. Fructose, which makes up half of table sugar and half of high-fructose corn syrup, is a special concern: in laboratory research it impairs mitochondrial function in the liver, and Lustig argues that similar effects reduce energy available to the brain. Insulin resistance has also been proposed as a contributor to depression.

Ketones, the fuel the body makes from fat when carbohydrate is scarce, offer an alternative. Lustig notes that the brain runs well on glucose but can run even better on ketones, and the ketogenic diet has treated childhood epilepsy for over a century. He reports that it has also been associated with fewer symptoms in schizophrenia and with less tremor in Parkinson's disease, and that it may slow cognitive decline in dementia and pre-dementia. Small trials of ketone-boosting MCT oil in people with mild cognitive impairment showed gains in brain energy and memory. I find it striking that conditions as different as epilepsy, psychiatric illness, and neurodegeneration have all been reported to improve with the same change in fuel; this suggests that how we feed the brain matters broadly. Whatever else a ketogenic diet changes, it removes sugar. I am not suggesting that everyone adopt a keto diet. Even Lustig admits that researchers do not yet know which element does the work: the ketones, lower insulin, the type of fat, or the absence of sugar. But the pattern is consistent enough that I am persuaded that reducing sugar is the best approach for a clear, sharp mind.

One clarification matters here: the target is *refined* sugar and carbohydrate, not fruit. Whole fruit comes with fiber that slows absorption, while juice strips that fiber away. As Lustig puts it, "eat your fruit — don't drink it."

I think most people intuitively know sugar is bad for them. They say, "I know it's bad for me but I'm eating it anyway." If we want to live our best, most authentic lives, we must steer away from that attitude.

### Replace sugar with nutrient density

As you remove sugar, it helps to know what to replace it with. What I advocate is maximizing the nutrient density of your food, not its sugar. Instead of seeking treats, look for ways to enrich your nutrient intake. A helpful, if imperfect, resource is the [Aggregate Nutrient Density Index](https://www.simplyfresh.info/andi-guide/), a ranking of foods by nutrient content. Build meals around the foods that score highest, such as leafy greens, and let berries replace dessert. I would also encourage anyone to consider eliminating sugary cereals, fruit juices, sodas, and desserts.

Swapping sugar for artificial sweeteners is not the answer. They have no calories, but they may not be harmless. In a well-known [2014 study](https://weizmann-usa.org/news-media/news-releases/gut-bacteria-artificial-sweeteners-and-glucose-intolerance/), mice given artificial sweeteners developed glucose intolerance through changes in their gut bacteria, and a small human trial found something similar in some volunteers. In 2023 the World Health Organization [advised against using non-sugar sweeteners](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240073616) for weight control, citing no long-term benefit and possible links to diabetes and heart disease. The evidence is still developing, but the better goal is to lose the expectation that everything must be sweet.

It is possible to adapt. After sugar largely leaves the diet, lower-sugar foods begin to taste better, and many sugary things simply taste "too sweet."

### A cultural note

Even many faith traditions have a culture around sugar; giving away sugary treats is a common way to show affection. Expressing love to our neighbors matters. I only wish we could find ways to express friendship that do not involve sugar.

### Every zone

I believe that more responsible attitudes toward food will someday become commonplace. Isaiah's prophecy of the Millennium describes a time when:

>Never again will there be in it [in "Jerusalem"] an infant who lives but a few days, or an old man who does not live out his years; the one who dies at a hundred will be thought a mere child; the one who fails to reach a hundred will be considered accursed.  
>
>—Isaiah 65:20 (NIV)

I read this as an application, not necessarily a forecast. But to see this vision fulfilled, the blue zones would have to become every zone. We should eat like we want to live to a hundred, and I think we know intuitively what that means.

*This post describes my personal philosophy and experience. It is not medical advice; talk with your doctor before making major dietary changes, especially if you take medication for diabetes or another condition.*
